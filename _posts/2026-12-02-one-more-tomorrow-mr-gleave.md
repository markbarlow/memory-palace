In our first English class at the start of second year seniors, Mr Gleave introduced himself by lifting a large dictionary in the air to celebrate the English language's claim to more than half a million words. He noted in passing that this was considerably more than one might find in French, then thunked the tome down on his desk. There was no other teacher's introduction to match this one.

He always wore an off-white shirt, a tie of muted colours, a grey or brown suit, and comfortable Clarks shoes. He didn't try hard to be liked by students or to entertain. He was wry and understated, while patently passionate about the written or spoken word. He seemed to enjoy the class interaction, even with a bunch of 12-year-old boys. A strain of competitiveness with all things French was a constant throughout the year. And as a result of some or all of these factors we warmed to him, and always behaved well in his class.

While some teachers would take chalk in hand to deliver lines to our exercise books, he preferred to dictate. In doing so, small white balls of saliva would collect in the corners of his lips. He would also regularly get the whole class to read, the role of narrator rotating from pupil to pupil. Fingers would move word by word along the page. When the mood took him, he would bring the narrator to stand at the front of the class. 

During a read through of *Henry V* I was called forward for the tennis balls scene. A tennis ball was produced from a desk drawer and offered as a prop. The mini thespian in me was activated, and I launched into the lines with appropriate sarcasm and indignation: 

*We are glad the Dauphin is so pleasant with us.*  
*His present and your pains we thank you for.*  
*When we have matched our rackets to these balls,*  
*We will in France, by God’s grace, play a set*  
*Shall strike his father’s crown into the hazard.*

As I delivered the final line, I slammed the ball into the floor with a flourish. The ball bounced up and off the wall then shot back centimetres from my face, flying into the second row of desks, to the amusement of both class and teacher. 

This would be bettered by a bemused Nick G when we came to read *Macbeth*, Act V Scene V, featuring Macbeth's famous soliloquy after the death of Lady Macbeth. Two tomorrows Nick could take, but three was just too much. Was this a typo or mistake? He started well with a confident opening:  

*Tomorrow...*  

And followed it steadily with the second mention:  

*...and tomorrow...*

Only to stumble at the third, delivered quizzically with rising intonation, effectively taking the liberty to add a question mark on to the end of the line:  

*...and tomorrow?*  

Epizeuxis be damned! Macbeth's nihilistic despair instantly and inadvertently turned into comic relief. Mr Gleave could not contain his delight, applauding this unique and surely never-repeated confounded delivery of the famous line.
